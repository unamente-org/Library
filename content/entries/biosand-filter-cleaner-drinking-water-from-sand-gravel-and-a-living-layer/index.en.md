### The story

In 1989 David Manz, an engineer teaching at the University of Calgary in Canada, started work on a water filter small enough for one home. He looked at older sand filters to see why they had failed. He found two problems. There was no easy way to stop and start the water. And the living layer that does much of the cleaning needed protecting.

He worked on it with a group of his engineering students, at first with his own money. He tested a prototype in 1991. In 1993 he and one of his graduate students took the filter to Nicaragua and put it in four homes. The next year, 60 filters went into homes in the town of Nandaime.

Manz patented the filter in 1995, but the Government of Alberta says he took a humanitarian rather than a business approach to it. In 2001 he co-founded CAWST, a nonprofit organisation, with Camille Dow Baker, an engineer who ran it as a volunteer for its first ten years. CAWST trains people to build the filter and gives the instructions away. By 2018, the Government of Alberta counted more than two million biosand filters built around the world.

### The problem it solves

Many families get their water from a river, a pond, a shallow well or a tank that is not safe to drink from. Germs in that water cause diarrhoea. Boiling works, but it costs fuel every day. A biosand filter cleans water with no fuel and nothing to buy once it is built.

### How it works

The filter is a tall box, usually concrete, filled with layers of fine sand and gravel. You pour water into the top. It sinks slowly through the sand and comes out of a pipe into a clean container.

A layer of water, 4 to 6 cm deep, always sits on top of the sand when no water is flowing. In that layer, and in the top of the sand, a living film of tiny organisms grows. It is called the biolayer. Germs from the dirty water get trapped in the sand, eaten by the organisms in the biolayer, or simply die. CAWST describes four ways the filter removes them: trapping, sticking to the sand, being eaten, and dying off.

### What you need

**To build one:** a concrete box cast in a mould, or another container that is waterproof, rustproof and safe for drinking water. Sand and gravel that have been sorted to the right sizes and washed. A diffuser plate so the water you pour does not stir up the top of the sand. A pipe and a clean container with a lid for the filtered water. The steps are in CAWST's construction manual, which is free and linked below.

**The cost.** CAWST's 2025 sheet puts it at 12 to 100 US dollars, depending on what the filter body is made of and what labor costs. Its 2009 sheet gave 12 to 40 US dollars for a concrete filter. Neither figure includes training, transport or teaching families to use the filter. After that there is little to buy: the sand never needs replacing, but the lid and diffuser may need replacing every few years. CAWST expects a filter to last ten years or more.

**The work.** Building one is real work: mixing and casting concrete, and sorting and washing a lot of sand and gravel. Using it is daily work too. CAWST's 2025 sheet says a household filter treats up to 48 liters a day, poured in as one to four batches spread through the day, and the water comes out at 0.29 to 0.6 liters a minute. After each batch you wait at least an hour before pouring in more. Someone has to fetch and carry all of that water.

### How to use it

1.  **Let it ripen.** A new filter does not clean well at first. The biolayer has to grow. CAWST says this can take up to 30 days, depending on the water and how often the filter is used. You can use the water during this time, but disinfect it after it comes out of the filter, for example by boiling it.

2.  **Pour in water every day, in batches.** CAWST recommends a rest of 6 to 12 hours between batches. Never less than one hour. Never more than 48 hours.

3.  **Use the same water source.** Switching sources can make the filter work less well for a while.

4.  **Never put chlorine in the filter.** Do not pour in chlorinated water, even water that is chlorinated only some of the time. CAWST says chlorine makes the filter work less well. If you also want to disinfect, do it after the water comes out.

5.  **Strain very muddy water first.** If the water is very cloudy (CAWST's threshold is more than 50 NTU, a measure of cloudiness), let it settle or pour it through a cloth before it goes in.

6.  **Clean it only when it slows down.** When the flow gets too slow for the family's needs, stir the top of the sand by hand and scoop out the dirty water. CAWST calls this "swirl and dump". It disturbs the biolayer, so the filter cleans less well for a while afterwards.

7.  **Keep the clean water clean.** Collect it straight into a covered container and keep the outlet pipe clean.

### What to expect, and what not to expect

**It cut diarrhoea in a careful trial.** In 2005 and 2006, researchers tested the filter in Bonao, in the Dominican Republic. They picked at random which households got a filter: 75 homes with one and 79 without completed the study. Over six months, the families with a filter had about half the odds of diarrhoea (0.53) compared with families without one.

**But not in the rainy months.** In the dry season the filter homes did much better (0.40). In the rainy season the difference was small (0.86) and could have been chance. The researchers think the main reason is that diarrhoea also fell in the homes without a filter during the rainy months. Either way, this trial cannot promise the same benefit all year round.

**The water got cleaner, but not as clean as in the lab.** In the trial, water from filter homes had 11 E. coli bacteria per 100 millilitres, against 19 in homes without a filter. The researchers say the filter removed about 83% of E. coli in their study, less than the 90 to 99% usually found in earlier lab and field studies.

**What CAWST's own summary says it removes.** CAWST's 2009 fact sheet says the filter is very effective against bacteria, protozoa and worms (CAWST's figure for worms is an assumption, not a measurement), and against cloudiness. It says the filter is only somewhat effective against viruses. Quoting earlier studies, it gives up to 96.5% of bacteria removed in the lab, more than 99.9% of protozoa in the lab, 70 to more than 99% of viruses in the lab, and 87.9 to 98.5% of bacteria in homes. For cloudiness it gives 95% in the lab and 85% in homes. We have not yet checked those earlier studies ourselves.

### When it does not work

**It does not remove most chemicals dissolved in the water, including salt.** CAWST says it does remove iron, and that a modified design, the Kanchan Arsenic Filter, can remove arsenic. If your water has a chemical problem, the standard filter is not the answer on its own.

**It is not safe straight away.** For up to a month after it is built, the biolayer is still growing. Disinfect the water during that time.

**It does not stop all viruses.** CAWST rates it only somewhat effective against them.

**It is not meant for chlorinated water.** CAWST's 2025 sheet says a biosand filter is not the right choice where the water is chlorinated, even if only sometimes.

**It cannot be moved.** Once installed, the filter stays where it is. An empty concrete body weighs 70 to 135 kg, so it does not suit families who move often.

**The trial did not show a benefit in the rainy season.** See above. Plan for that, especially if floods or heavy rain bring dirtier water.

**The trial relied on what families reported, and nobody checked how the filters were used.** The researchers say families with a filter may have reported less illness simply because they had one, and they could not measure how well families used the filters. They also say the results may not apply everywhere.

**Clean water can get dirty again.** Water stored for a long time in the home can lose its quality, as the trial's authors note. A clean container with a lid matters as much as the filter.

**It does not work well if it is used badly.** Pouring in water too often, leaving it unused for days, switching water sources or stirring the sand too often all weaken the biolayer.

### Common mistakes, and how to avoid them

Drinking the water before the filter has ripened. Leaving the filter unused for more than two days. Cleaning the sand when the flow is still fine. Using a dirty or open container for the filtered water. Using sand that is too fine, which makes the water barely drip. Putting a tap or hose on the outlet pipe: the outlet sets the water level inside the filter, and a tap changes it. Using the same container to fetch dirty water and to collect filtered water, which makes the clean water dirty again. The manual explains how to get each of these right.

### Where it has been used

The Government of Alberta reported in 2018 that CAWST and its partners had helped build 910 000 biosand filters in 74 countries. Samaritan's Purse and other groups trained by Manz built about a million more. Early filters went into homes in Nicaragua. CAWST's co-founder writes that Manz had also run filter programmes through other organisations in Vietnam, Brazil and Kenya.

### An open question we would like answered

Does the filter protect families in the rainy season, when floods and run-off make water dirtier? The best trial we found could not show it, and its authors think the reason was something happening in the homes without filters, not a failure of the filter. If you have measured how biosand filters do in the wet season or after floods, we would like to hear from you.

Two smaller questions sit beside it. What is the cheapest reliable way to add a second step, such as chlorine after filtering, for families whose water carries viruses? And how can a family tell, without a laboratory, that its own filter is still doing its job and not just letting water through?

### Credit and licence

David Manz designed the household biosand filter at the University of Calgary, starting in 1989, with help from his engineering students. One of his graduate students went with him to install the first filters in Nicaragua in 1993; we could not find that student's name. Camille Dow Baker co-founded CAWST with him and ran it for its first decade. CAWST developed the manual with WASH Skills Development Organization Cambodia. The Pan American Health Organization paid for the first trip to Nicaragua.

Manz patented the filter in 1995. A plastic version of the filter is patented and sold under licence; this entry is about the concrete filter, whose design CAWST gives away. Manz also had a company, Davnor, that sold the filter. Camille Dow Baker writes that they first looked to that company to spread it, and then decided that getting it to the people who needed it most required a nonprofit of its own. CAWST gives the instructions for the concrete filter away free, in several languages. UnaMente links to CAWST's page rather than copying the manual, because CAWST updates it and asks that it not be rehosted.
