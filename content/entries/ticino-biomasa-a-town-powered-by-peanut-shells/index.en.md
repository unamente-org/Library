Ticino is a small town in Argentina’s peanut country, and peanut processing leaves mountains of shells. Instead of treating the shells as waste, a local company built a power plant that burns them: the shells raise steam, the steam drives a turbine, and the turbine makes electricity — enough for far more households than the town itself holds. When Argentina’s national grid went dark in country-wide blackouts, Ticino kept its lights on.

**At a glance** Origin: Ticino, Córdoba Province, Argentina · Maturity: a commercial plant running since August 2018 · Scale: about 5 MW installed (4.63 MW by the operator's own figure); enough for about 8 000 homes, fed into the national grid · Licence: the plant is a private asset; the underlying idea is open.

**The story, and why it’s here.** This is a Lane 3 story because it is an industrial plant owned by a peanut-processing company, not a village build. What makes it worth telling is the move at its heart — turning a locally abundant crop residue into reliable power — and the resilience that came with it. The plant is rated at about 5 MW; it needs about 40 000 tonnes of peanut shell to produce 3 MW, and about 1 MW runs the company's own peanut plant. It was among the first projects in Córdoba awarded under RenovAr 2, Argentina's national renewable-energy auction: it bid US\$110 per megawatt-hour and holds a 20-year contract to supply the national grid, it makes enough for about 8 000 homes, far more than a town of about 3 000 people uses. When the national grid failed, in June 2019 and again on 1 March 2023, a protocol switched Ticino onto a closed circuit fed by the plant, and the town kept its lights on.

**What to take from it — honestly.** The transferable idea is matching a power plant to a local waste stream — peanut shells here, but rice husk, sawmill offcuts or bagasse elsewhere. That idea scales down in principle; this particular plant does not, because it is industrial and capital-intensive. Burning biomass is lower-carbon only when the residue would otherwise rot or be burned anyway, and it is not emission-free. The capacity and reach figures come from reporting and the company, not from an independent audit. When peanut shell runs short, the plant sometimes burns wood chip instead — the waste stream that makes it work is also its limit.

**Who made it.** Generación Ticino Biomasa, part of the peanut company Lorenzati, Ruetsch y Cía, with Fabio Bruschini as president.
