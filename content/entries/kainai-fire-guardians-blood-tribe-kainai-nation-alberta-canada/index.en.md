### The story

On Kainai land, fire was once part of caring for the country. Then it stopped. Alvin First Rider, Kainai's environmental manager, told The Narwhal that the last time he saw anyone burn was in the mid-1990s. He calls fire suppression "a colonial tactic."

First Rider worked for years to bring it back. In March 2025, Natural Resources Canada agreed to fund the Blood Tribe's fire guardianship work: 500 000 Canadian dollars from March 2025 to March 2028, under the title "Blood Tribe Fire Guardianship: Reigniting Kinship Relationships." The Narwhal reported it as the first fire guardians program in Canada. Nathan Provost was hired as its first coordinator.

In early May 2025, in Standoff, the Kainai fire department, the Fire Guardians, Waterton Lakes National Park and Blackfeet fire management from Montana came together to share knowledge and train, ending with a live burn near the powwow arbor. The Narwhal describes four days; the Blood Tribe's own site, a week. Before the fire, Elder Calvin Williams (Mah Tsa Oh Taan) made an offering of tobacco, and he and Elder Dennis Chief Calf (Naato kiinaamaahka) led a prayer. Dried cattails were used to set the fire. It was, in First Rider's account, the first cultural fire there in decades.

### The role it plays

For First Rider, bringing fire back is part of a larger restoration of the land. "The goal is to rematriate our connection with fire along with our bison," he told The Narwhal, describing fire's return alongside the return of bison and beaver. He rejects the old idea that these lands were wild and untended: his people managed them, he says, and that is what they are bringing back.

Provost wants the program to teach the community, give young people a role, and show that fire is not simply destructive.

First Rider also teaches with a Blackfoot story about Napi and the Sun's fire. As The Narwhal reports it, he uses the story to show fire's power and the need to follow protocol. The story is his people's to tell; the article carries it as he told it.

### How it works, and how the community keeps it alive

**It begins with ceremony.** The first burn opened with an offering and a prayer led by Elders, before anyone lit a fire.

**Old ways are being remembered.** First Rider told The Narwhal that fire keepers once carried hot coals in bison horns. The first fire was set with dried cattails.

**It is being rebuilt with neighbors, on Kainai terms.** Waterton's fire crew has helped build the program for about six years, according to The Narwhal. Parks Canada writes that in 2022, two weeks after a burn it ran with Kainai members, its staff joined Kainai fire practitioners on the Blood Timber Reserve, the first time it had supported a First Nation in bringing fire back beyond the lands Parks Canada runs. Waterton's fire management officer helped Kainai carry out its first two burns on the 1 940 hectare Timber Limits and helped several Kainai land management staff earn wildland firefighting certification. Sheldon Brewer, a fuels specialist who belongs to both the Blackfeet Nation and Kainai, came to share his experience; he said it was his first cultural burn using traditional methods.

**The next steps are its own people.** The Narwhal reports that the program's next phases are equipment for cultural burns and hiring community members who already carry fire knowledge.

### What to understand, and what not to assume

**This is living cultural property, not a blueprint.** The ceremony, the protocol and the knowledge of when and where to burn belong to Kainai and the Blackfoot Confederacy. This page describes them; it does not teach them, and nothing here is meant to be followed elsewhere.

**Cultural burning is not prescribed burning.** Parks Canada itself puts it this way: "Prescribed fire is a colonial adaptation of cultural burning." Cultural burns are led by Indigenous people for cultural purposes; this entry is about that practice, not about an agency's fire program.

**It is young and it is a revival.** Burning had stopped on Kainai land for about three decades. The program is funded for three years, and its next phases depend on equipment and paid people. Amy Cardinal Christianson, senior fire advisor with the Indigenous Leadership Initiative, told The Narwhal that funders often assume wages in Indigenous programs can be lower, and that guardians should be paid much more.
