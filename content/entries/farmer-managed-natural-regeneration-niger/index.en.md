### The story

In the early 1980s the farmland of south-central Niger looked, from the air and from the road, like bare sand with scrub on it. Those scrubby bushes turned out to be something else. In 1983 Tony Rinaudo, an Australian agronomist working in Maradi, recognized that what everyone was clearing and burning as worthless shrubs were living tree stumps, resprouting — an entire forest still rooted in the ground, cut off at the ankles every year. The following year the Maradi Integrated Development Project began working with farmers on protecting and pruning that regrowth instead of clearing it.

What happened next is the part worth paying attention to, and the part usually left out. The practice spread mostly farmer to farmer, across villages, for forty years, and the farmers did not merely adopt it — they adapted it, worked out which species suited which goals, and built the village-level agreements that made protecting regrowth possible at all. The Nigerien research team that reviewed the whole body of evidence in 2023 credits farmers with most of the adoption and most of the innovation. If one name has to be attached to this, attach it to the farmers of Maradi and Zinder. Rinaudo's contribution was seeing what was already there, which is not nothing — but it is not the same as inventing it.

The result is one of the largest documented land restorations anywhere. Since the mid-1980s, farmers in Maradi, Zinder and Tahoua have regreened about five million hectares of farmland, according to the 2023 review — with roughly 200 million trees added across Maradi and Zinder alone. Tropenbos International's policy brief summarizing the same review gives larger figures: six million hectares, and the regrowth of at least 250 million trees. We give both, because the two documents differ and we have not found an explanation for the difference. Not planted. Released.

### The problem it solves

Farmland in the Sahel that has lost its trees loses more than shade. It loses the leaf litter that feeds the soil, the roots that hold it, and the canopy that keeps the ground from cooking in the sun. Seedlings fail, yields fall, and firewood has to be walked further for every year. Replanting at that scale costs money nobody has, needs nurseries, and most seedlings die. FMNR addresses the same problem from the other end: use the root systems that are already alive underground.

### What you need

Nothing you have to buy. No nursery, no seedlings, no inputs. A cutting tool, knowledge of which stumps are worth keeping, and — this is the real requirement — an agreement with your neighbors about livestock.

The cost is not money, it is sustained work and sustained discipline. Selecting and tagging stems, pruning them every few months, and protecting the regrowth from grazing animals and fire across years, not weeks. A practice that depends on nobody's goats eating your regrowth is a social undertaking before it is a technical one, and the protection has to hold season after season for the trees to reach a size where they can look after themselves.

### How to do it

The current field manual from World Vision Australia sets the practice out in five steps. The detail belongs in the manual itself, which is free to download and linked below — we link rather than reproduce it, because it is its authors' work and they deserve the visit.

1. **Find.** With the community, choose which tree species you want and why — fodder, firewood, fruit, soil, windbreak. Then survey the land for living stumps and seedlings, and select and tag the best ones.
2. **Prune.** On each stump, keep the three to five strongest, straightest stems and remove the rest, so the plant puts its energy upward rather than into a thicket.
3. **Protect.** Shield the regrowth from livestock, fire and damage — mainly through community agreements, "social fencing," rather than physical fences nobody can afford.
4. **Grow.** Keep pruning every two to six months so the main stems grow strong and straight.
5. **Use** (the manual calls this step Utilise). Harvest sustainably once the trees can bear it — for example one stem per tree per year on a rotation — so the trees keep producing instead of being cleared again.

### What to expect — and what not to expect

**When it works well.** Across the studies it gathered, the 2023 review found FMNR raised crop yields by anything from about 31 to 350 kilograms per hectare. That wide range is the honest summary: the gain depends on the place, the species, and how old and how dense the trees are. Within it, millet grown in *Faidherbia albida* parkland produced about 690 kilograms per hectare against about 350 kilograms per hectare on control plots in one Nigerien study — close to double. More conservatively, FMNR stands older than six years have been measured adding roughly 120 to 200 kilograms per hectare, varying with species, tree age and density; one study puts the range for millet at 120 to 209.

The effect that matters most shows up in bad years rather than good ones. In the drought year of 2011, Kantché department produced a cereal surplus of almost 13 000 metric tons.

**The ground stops cooking — and this is narrower than it sounds.** Researchers at the ICRISAT Sahelian Center at Sadoré, south of Niamey, measured soil temperature at two centimeters' depth near a *Faidherbia* tree through the 1990 season. Near the tree, maximum soil temperature was reduced by up to 10 °C depending on position and on timing relative to rain: about 40 °C under the canopy against 45 to 50 °C outside it before a storm, and about 35 °C against about 45 °C after one. In a companion experiment using a shade fence, mean daytime soil surface temperature was 43.3 °C at the fence base against 46.6 °C fully exposed, where the soil often passed 50 °C. Remarkably, the tree's canopy was nearly leafless at the time and still intercepted about half the incoming radiation.

In that trial the difference was not a matter of degree. Millet grew best in partial shade and **failed outright in full exposure** — plants three and a half meters from the shade fence died within ten days of emerging.

**Read that claim carefully, because it is easy to misread.** What was reduced is *soil surface* temperature. The same authors note that earlier work found the tree's effect on *air* temperature to be small. If you are hoping for shade that makes the working day cooler to stand in, this is not that. What it is, is ground that does not kill seedlings.

**And this particular finding is the weakest evidence in this entry.** One site, one season, one mature tree and three transects. The main shade experiment used an artificial fence rather than a tree, deliberately, so that the tree's soil-fertility effect would not contaminate the result. The authors are explicit that the lethal-temperature mechanism is a hypothesis they have not demonstrated, that the best canopy density is unknown, and that 1990 rainfall was 400 mm against a long-term mean of about 560 mm. Everything else in this entry rests on a body of work; this rests on one careful study.

**Other things the trees are doing.** Less water evaporates from the soil under FMNR than from bare plots, and shrubs have been shown to lift water from depth. The shade is not the whole mechanism, and in some settings it may not be the main one.

**It does not solve hunger, and we are not going to imply that it does.** The 2023 review states plainly that even with FMNR, cereal yields in Niger remain too low for a population growing at 3.9% a year. This is a real improvement on a desperate baseline. It is not food security, and anyone selling it as such is selling something.

**Conditions it needs.** Something alive to regenerate from — living stumps, or a seed bank in the soil. Where land has been fully cleared for decades, there is nothing to release and planting is the only option. It also needs protection from free-grazing livestock, which in practice means a community grazing agreement. That is an arrangement between people, not a technique, and it is the part that most often fails.

**It needs farmers to believe the trees are theirs.** Farmers in Niger have perceived a right to the trees on their own land since the late 1980s. In 2020 Niger adopted a decree regulating the practice, Décret n° 2020-602/PRN/ME/SU/DD of 30 July 2020, and according to the review it states that planted or regenerated trees belong to the producer. The review expects that to encourage further investment. There is a striking comparison here that should be handled carefully: northern Nigeria has similar rainfall, similar soils, similar population density and the same Hausa population — and far fewer trees. The review's explanation is that farmers there do not feel they own them. **The review also states that no study has tested this.** It is the authors' hypothesis, and a plausible one, not a finding.

**It can go backwards.** Tree loss has been concentrated where village management committees were absent or where there was local conflict. The standing risks named in the review are weak local governance, poor coordination between the organizations involved, and a lack of maintenance and ownership by the people the projects were meant to serve.

**More trees is not always better.** Some farmers report that high tree density cuts their yields through shade — which sits consistently with the 1992 study's own unanswered question about what canopy density is actually optimal.

**Common failures, and how to avoid them.** Pruning too hard or too rarely — the stems need the regular cycle, not one heroic session. Leaving regrowth unprotected for a single bad season, which can undo years. Treating the grazing agreement as a formality rather than the load-bearing part of the practice. And letting density rise without thinning, on the assumption that more trees must be better.

### Where it has been replicated

FMNR is promoted in Ethiopia, Kenya, Ghana, Myanmar and across Spanish-speaking countries, and the field manual exists in several languages. But the measured evidence base is overwhelmingly Nigerien, and we are not going to present promotion as proof.

One study from Ethiopia's Central Rift Valley reports that *Faidherbia albida* buffers wheat against climatic extremes, with midday air temperature about 6 °C lower under the trees and soil nitrogen 35 to 55% higher near the crowns. We have read only its abstract — the full text is paywalled — so we cite it as a lead rather than as evidence, and we note that it measures air temperature where the Nigerien study measured soil temperature, and reaches a different-sounding conclusion about air. Those two results need somebody to read both papers properly before anyone claims they agree.

### An open question we would like answered

Two, really. *Faidherbia albida* has a reverse phenology — leafless in the rainy season, in leaf in the dry — which is precisely what lets a crop grow underneath it. Whether that behavior, and the benefit that follows from it, holds outside the Sahel is something the evidence base cannot currently answer, because almost all of the evidence is from one country. That looks to us like an accident of where the research happened rather than a limit of the tree.

The second is sharper, and it has been sitting unanswered since 1992: what canopy density best trades the temperature reduction the crop needs against the light the crop also needs? The farmers who report that too many trees cut their yields are describing the same question from the other side. As far as we can find, nobody has answered it. If you have measured this, we would like to hear from you.

### Credit and license

This practice belongs to the farmers of Maradi, Zinder and Tahoua, who spread and adapted it among themselves over four decades. Tony Rinaudo recognized in 1983 that the shrubs were living stumps; the Maradi Integrated Development Project introduced the approach in 1984. Neither invented what the farmers then built.

The 2023 state-of-knowledge review is the work of Tougiani Abasse, Moussa Massaoudou, Habou Rabiou, Soumana Idrissa and Dan Guimbo Iro — a Nigerien team writing about Niger, which is the right way round and worth saying.
