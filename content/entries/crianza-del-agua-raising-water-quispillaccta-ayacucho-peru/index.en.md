### The story

In Quispillaccta, water is not treated as a resource. In the words of the community's own account, "el agua es una persona con saber propio": water is a person with its own knowledge. People do not manage water; they raise it, as they raise crops and animals, and it raises them in return.

The Asociación Bartolomé Aripaylla was founded in Quispillaccta in 1991, as the years of political violence were ending, to strengthen Andean culture and farming on the community's own terms. By 1994, in the neighborhood of Tuco, water was so short in the dry season that it was causing disputes, and the old ways of caring for water were being forgotten. Talking it through, people decided to bring that culture back. That year, working together, people built a dike on a low edge of what is now the Apacheta lagoon. It was the first.

From there the work spread. ABA's own figures for 2016 range from about 77 to more than 100 lagoons, depending on what is counted, and in 2013 the district of Chuschi declared 51 of them natural heritage.

### The role it plays

The lagoons hold rain where it falls, high on the mountain. The water soaks slowly into the ground and comes out lower down as springs, which are the community's main source of water for people, animals and fields. ABA describes the chain of lagoons as standing in for the snowcaps that melted away.

ABA's own account reports new springs appearing below the lagoons, existing springs flowing more strongly, streams that no longer dry up completely in the dry season, and new wetlands forming. It gives an estimate of the water stored: about 1 700 000 cubic meters on its website, and about 1 200 000 cubic meters in its 2016 chapter. Both are ABA's own figures; we have found no one else who measured them.

### How it works, and how the community keeps it alive

**It starts with conversation.** Authorities, community members and the *yachaq*, the wise people who lead the rituals, walk the high ground together, sharing coca leaves, to choose where a lagoon could be founded.

**Permission comes before work.** Before anything is built, the community consults the *apus*, the guardian mountains, in a ritual called *Tapukuy*. ABA writes that the *yachaq*, musicians, young people and children are the ones called to take part, because they are the ones the deities listen to most.

**The work is shared.** Lagoons are made in the dry season, by families working together in *minka*, collective work organized by the traditional authorities, the *varayuq*: men, women, young people and children, with picks, shovels and wheelbarrows, and at times machinery lent by local government. In ABA's chapter, a new lagoon took on average about 510 days of communal work, which the chapter values at 20 400 soles, besides about 8 395 soles of outside money. That is hard physical work at high altitude, given by the families themselves.

**The water is raised, not just stored.** Algae and fish are brought from older, stronger lagoons to help a new one settle. *Putaqa* and other plants that ABA calls "mothers of the water" are planted where springs come up below. Banks are protected against silt. Grazing is limited by zone, herds were reduced, and the native grass was brought back on worn ground to hold the soil.

**It takes years.** "La crianza del agua de lluvia no es para desesperados," ABA writes: raising rainwater is not for the impatient. Its website says the first year's water soaks the surrounding ground and the water starts to seep down from the second year; its chapter says a lagoon can fill from the third year.

**It is passed on.** About thirty *yachaq* lead the rituals and carry the knowledge. Children and young people are given the task of looking after new lagoons, so that, in ABA's words, they grow attached to and respectful of nature. The local preschool writes the festive cleaning of the canals, *Yarqa Aspiy*, into its yearly plan. Communities exchange visits, and the community keeps watch over its lagoons, recording new springs and how much water there is.

### What to understand, and what not to assume

**This is living cultural property, not a blueprint.** Raising water in Quispillaccta is inseparable from the people who do it: their rituals, their authorities, their shared work and their way of seeing water as a living being. This page describes it; it does not teach it, and nothing here is meant to be followed elsewhere.

**ABA calls its experience highly replicable, on its own terms.** That is the association's own view, tied in its chapter to low cost, local materials and a widely shared Andean culture. It is not an invitation from UnaMente to copy the practice. Anyone who wants to learn from it should turn to ABA and the community.

**It has not been easy.** ABA's own chapter lists the troubles: early distrust of works built without concrete; little political will from local government; contests run by the municipality and another organization that encouraged competition between families and caused tension; evangelical members who at first moved into the community's leadership and sought to steer community life toward a Christian community, and who, ABA writes, ended up joining the work; disputes over the boundaries between neighborhoods; and uncertainty when mining companies arrived. Families who did not reduce their herds had animals auctioned. Much of the cash came from outside funders, among them organizations in Germany and Spain and the United Nations World Food Programme.

**Read the figures as ABA's own.** They come from an advocate's account, and its own pages do not agree on how many lagoons there are or how much water they hold. We show both.

### Custodians, and how this entry was made

This knowledge is held by the Indigenous community of Quispillaccta. The custodian-led organization to turn to is the **Asociación Bartolomé Aripaylla**, through the [contact page on its own site](https://abaayacucho.org/contacto). We name no private individuals' contact details.

This entry was written from ABA's own website and from its own chapter, "Crianza del Agua y los Criadores de Quispillaccta", written by Marcela Machaca of ABA with testimony from community members in Tuco, Huertahuasi, Puncupata, Yuracc Cruz and Unión Potrero. Both were read in Castellano; the quotations are translated by us. It is not an endorsement of UnaMente by ABA or anyone else.
