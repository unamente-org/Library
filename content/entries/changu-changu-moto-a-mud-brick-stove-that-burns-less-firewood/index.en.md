**Category:** Energy · **Origin:** Malawi, by Ripple Africa, adapted from a 16-brick design by Dr Larry Winiarski · **Maturity:** Scaled (one operator) · **Trust tier:** Limited · **License:** Ripple Africa publishes a free build guide and invites people to copy the design · **Entry date:** 27 November 2026 · **Documented by:** UnaMente

### The story

In rural Malawi, most families cook on a three-stone fire: three stones, a pot on top, and wood burning in the open. Ripple Africa, a UK-registered charity established in 2003, helps communities build a stove from 26 mud bricks instead. It is called the Changu Changu Moto, which means "Fast Fast Fire" in Chichewa. Ripple Africa says it is an adaptation of a 16-brick design by Dr Larry Winiarski, specially modified for Malawi after trials and feedback from the people using it. Trained volunteers in each village build it with each household, inside the kitchen.

### The problem it solves

A three-stone fire burns a lot of wood. Ripple Africa says a three-stone fire uses three to four bundles of wood a week, and that the wood takes three to five hours a week to collect. Where trees are scarce, or wood has to be bought, that is a heavy cost.

### How it works

The fire burns inside a small mud-brick box instead of in the open, and the heat goes to two pots at once, each resting on mud pot rests (the guide makes eight in all). It is fed with small pieces of wood. The design has no chimney.

### What you need

- 26 small bricks, burnt or unburnt. The guide says to make 30, and gives the ideal size as 22.5 × 11 × 6.5 cm.
- Mortar: 7.5 liters of clay soil and 7.5 liters of sandy soil, mixed with about 5 liters of water.
- A 20-liter bucket, a 5-liter cooking pot, a hoe and a trowel.
- A place in the kitchen, under a roof.

**The work.** The bricks have to be made and dried before anything else. Ripple Africa's page says the stove "takes just one hour to make"; it does not say whether that includes making the bricks, and the guide gives no time. The mud then needs two to three days to dry before the stove is used. Nobody has measured how long making the bricks takes. After that, the stove needs regular upkeep (see What keeps it running).

### How to do it

Follow Ripple Africa's free Step-by-Step Guide, linked below. Ripple Africa says anyone can build one and encourages people to copy the design. In outline: make and dry the bricks, mix the mortar, prepare the place, build the stove, plaster mud around the outside, make the pot rests (the guide says each should be the size of a Coke bottle), and let it dry.

### What to expect, and what not to expect

**Less firewood, by Ripple Africa's count.** Ripple Africa says a household that cooks on the stove uses one bundle of wood a week instead of three to four (two to three bundles fewer, by our arithmetic), and that this cuts household wood use by 67%.

**One study, and why it is weak.** Researchers from Malawi's College of Forestry and Wildlife weighed the wood used by 100 households in Usisya, in Nkhata Bay district, after they had the stove: about 30 kg a week. The households were measured in 2013; the study was published in 2015. But the "before" figure they compared it with came from Ripple Africa's own records, and there was no comparison group. So this is not an independent measurement of the saving, and its result is Ripple Africa's figure again. That is why we rate this entry Limited.

**No smoke or health claim.** This stove has no chimney, so whatever smoke it makes stays in the kitchen. Nobody has measured how much smoke the families breathe. The 2015 study's authors say the same: before the stove is promoted on a large scale, they write, its emissions of harmful pollutants should be measured. For a stove with a chimney, and what research found about smoke and health, see our Improved Wood Cookstove entry.

### When it does not work

Nothing here has been measured. These limits come from the design, not from evidence:
- It is built into one kitchen, so it does not move with a family.
- It is made of mud, so it needs a roof over it in the rains.
- It cooks two pots at a time.
- It has no chimney, so the kitchen still has smoke.

### What keeps it running

**Regular upkeep is part of the deal.** The guide says to re-plaster mud onto the outside and remake the pot rests every two to four weeks. That is the household's own time, mud and water, between 13 and 26 times a year by our arithmetic. Nobody has measured how long each time takes.

**Repairs.** Between those, Ripple Africa says the stove "can be easily repaired by householders when broken", and that householders are taught to care for, repair and even replace their stoves. There are no bought parts.

**People stop using it if nobody follows up.** Ripple Africa says that when it first expanded quickly, some households stopped using their stoves a few months after they were built. Often the cause was simple: the large branches used on an open fire are too big for this stove. Getting the stoves back in use took several follow-up visits. Its coordinators now return to each household several times over two years, and it says: "We measure not just the number of stoves built, but the number of stoves still in use." It publishes no count of stoves found broken or out of use. Nobody has studied what happens after the visits stop. Still in use, years later: not measured.

### Where it has been used

Malawi. Ripple Africa says it has helped communities build more than 125 000 of these stoves (its own figure, as read on 26 November 2026). The 2015 study was in Nkhata Bay district.

### Credit and license

**Ripple Africa** adapted the stove for Malawi and spreads it through trained village volunteers. It is based on a **16-brick design by Dr Larry Winiarski**, whose rocket-stove principles, set out at the Aprovecho Research Center, are described in our Improved Wood Cookstove entry. The 2015 wood study is by **Gift Chimulu, Dan Ndalowa and Edward Missanjo** of Malawi's College of Forestry and Wildlife.

Ripple Africa publishes its build guide free and invites people to copy the design. UnaMente links to the guide and does not copy it.
