**Category:** Water and soil · **Origin:** highland communities of the Peruvian Andes · **Maturity:** Scaled · **Trust tier:** Moderate, with one weaker claim marked below · **License:** Build manual free to download from desco · **Entry date:** 26 November 2026 · **Documented by:** UnaMente

### The story

High in the Peruvian Andes, the rain comes in one season, and then comes a long dry season. Herding and farming communities there describe this as ancestral knowledge, and Peru's 2019 law speaks of "las técnicas ancestrales" of sowing and harvesting water. One of the ways they slow the rain down is the qocha (also written qucha or cocha): a small earth dam closing a natural hollow, so that rain collects there instead of running off the mountain.

In 1991 the Machaca sisters, agronomists from the community of Quispillaccta in Ayacucho, founded the Asociación Bartolomé Aripaylla (ABA). One account names all three sisters, Magdalena, Marcela and Lidia, as founders; another names Marcela and Magdalena. From 1994 they and their community began bringing back small lagoons in the locality of Tuco. For Quispillaccta, this work is part of a living practice of its own, *crianza del agua*, the raising of water. That practice belongs to the community. UnaMente documents it separately, from what the community's own association has published: see [Crianza del Agua (Raising Water)](https://library.unamente.org/library/crianza-del-agua-raising-water-quispillaccta-ayacucho-peru/). This entry is only about the earth dam itself.

Other groups have since written the technique down and spread it. desco, a Peruvian development organization, published a free build manual in 2008. PACC Perú, a climate adaptation program working with Peru's environment ministry, helped communities in Cusco and Apurímac build 146 qochas in 2012 and 2013. In 2019 Peru passed a law declaring "siembra y cosecha de agua", sowing and harvesting water, a matter of national interest.

### The problem it solves

In a long dry season, or in a drought year such as El Niño can bring, water is short for people, animals and pasture. Rain that runs straight off the slopes in the wet months is water lost. A qocha keeps some of it on the mountain, either to use directly or to soak slowly into the ground.

### How it works

A qocha closes a natural hollow with a low earth dike. Rain collects behind it. Larger ones also have a spillway, to let extra water out safely in a storm, and an outlet pipe, to let people draw water when they need it. The desco manual shows which parts each size needs.

PACC describes three kinds, depending on the ground under the water:
- some let the water soak into the ground ("siembra de agua", sowing water);
- some hold it, because the ground does not let it through ("almacenamiento", storage);
- and some do both ("función mixta").

**How big?** For small community ponds (*espejos de agua*), the desco manual gives a dike 6 meters wide at the base, 1.5 meters high and 1.5 meters wide at the top. The same manual allows up to 5 meters of height only for larger small dams (*microrrepresas*), which are a bigger job. The qochas PACC helped build held about 570 cubic meters each, on average (our figure, from PACC's totals: 83 177 cubic meters in 146 qochas).

**A warning about the word.** In Peru today, "qocha" also names much larger dams built by the state fund Sierra Azul. For example, Agraria.pe reported in October 2025 that Sierra Azul would build 13 qochas in Junín and Pasco for 5.9 million soles, storing more than a million cubic meters. That is a different scale of project. This entry is about the small ones a community builds itself.

### What you need

- A natural hollow in the right place, where the ends of the dike can rest on earth, not rock (see below).
- Earth and stone from the site, basic tools, and for larger dams an outlet pipe.
- The agreement of your neighbors and of the water authority, before you dig.
- A fence or wall, so animals do not trample the pasture that feeds the qocha.
- The desco manual, which is free and linked below. It shows how to choose the site and build the dike, and which parts each size needs. We link to it rather than copy its drawings, because it is desco's work.

**The cost.** PACC estimated that its qochas cost about 3 soles per cubic meter of water stored, against about 142 soles for a concrete reservoir and 10 soles for an earth dam. These are the program's own estimates. In total, PACC's 146 qochas cost about 250 000 soles, or about 1 712 soles each (our figure, from PACC's totals).

**The work.** Most of that cost was people's work. PACC says that 68% of the investment was the families' and communities' own labor and tools, and 32% came from the program. That is heavy digging, carrying and building, high in the mountains. We did not find a figure for how many days of work one qocha takes. The desco manual does give figures for protecting the pasture around it: about 15 days of work per hectare for a wire fence, 20 for a mesh fence, and about 4 meters of double stone wall per worker per day.

### How to do it

The desco manual is the guide. In outline:
1. **Choose the site with the community.** A natural hollow that collects water, on earth, not rock.
2. **Agree on who uses the water,** and write it down.
3. **Ask the water authority for permission** before building.
4. **Build the dike,** and for a larger dam its spillway and outlet pipe, as the manual shows.
5. **Fill it only halfway in the first year,** while the dike settles.
6. **Protect the pasture around it** from grazing animals.

### What to expect, and what not to expect

**It stores water.** That part is not in doubt. A sealed hollow with a sound dike holds rain into the dry season.

**Springs below may come back, but nobody has measured how much.** PACC reports that, below the dams it helped build, "diversos manantiales y fuentes de agua suelen aparecer o recuperar su caudal" (several springs and water sources often appear or recover their flow). That comes from what families and field staff saw. The same brief says plainly: "aún se desconoce el volumen que pueden llegar a infiltrar estos sistemas" (the volume these systems can soak into the ground is still unknown). It calls its supporting studies preliminary. **This is the weakest claim in this entry.** Treat any promise of more spring water as hopeful, not proven.

**One qocha is not enough.** PACC says building them in large numbers "sí significaría una contribución significativa para la cuenca" (would make a significant contribution to the catchment). It also says these systems "por sí solas, no solucionarán los problemas del agua" (will not, on their own, solve water problems). PACC built them alongside pasture protection and other work.

### When it does not work

**Do not rest the dike on rock.** The desco manual says the ends of the dike should rest on earth, not rock, because earth does not stick well to rock and the dike can leak at the join.

**Never build without your neighbors' agreement.** For small dams, the manual says: "No se pueden construir microrrepresas sin la aprobación de los propietarios circundantes a la laguna" (small dams cannot be built without the approval of the landholders around the lagoon). It also asks for written documents recording the agreement on who may use the water. We treat this as the rule for any qocha.

**Never build without permission.** The manual asks for the prior approval of the local irrigation authority.

**The water must not belong only to the landowner.** The manual says the work should avoid the owner of the land being the only one who benefits.

**Do not fill it all at once.** For small dams, the manual says to fill only to half of capacity in the first year, while the earth settles.

**It needs a hollow and the right ground.** Where there is no natural basin, or no earth to rest the dike on, this is the wrong tool.

**It is not a dam on its own.** PACC built its qochas together with protecting the pasture that feeds them, and the desco manual is as much about caring for the pastures as about the dam.

### What keeps it running

The upkeep that is written down is the pasture around the qocha: a fence or a stone wall to keep animals off, and the work figures for building them are given above. In the first year the qocha is filled only halfway while the dike settles. The communities do most of the work themselves: in PACC's program, 68% of the investment was their own labor and tools. Nobody we have read has documented how often a dike or spillway needs repair, who repairs it, or what happens to the qochas when a supporting program moves on. Still in use, years later: not measured.

### Where it has been used

Qochas are built across the Peruvian highlands. PACC Perú helped communities build 146 in the Huacrahuacho (Cusco) and Mollebamba (Apurímac) microcatchments in 2012 and 2013, including the community of Santa Rosa in Mollebamba. Quispillaccta in Ayacucho has worked with lagoons since 1994. Peru's state fund Sierra Azul has built much larger qochas across many regions; Agraria.pe reported 41 families for the Junín and Pasco project in October 2025, and 141 families for the same project in February 2026.

In 2019 Peru passed Law 30989, published on 23 July 2019. It declares "de interés nacional y necesidad pública la implementación de la siembra y cosecha de agua" (the implementation of sowing and harvesting water a matter of national interest and public need), together with "la difusión de las técnicas ancestrales" (spreading the ancestral techniques).

### An open question we would like answered

How much water does a qocha really give back to the springs below it? Families see springs return, but the program that built 146 of them says the volume is still unknown. If you have measured spring flow below qochas, before and after, we would like to hear from you.

And a second question: does the technique work in other high, dry mountains with a long dry season? The evidence we found is almost all from Peru. We cannot tell whether that is because it only suits Peru's highlands, or only because Peru is where people have written about it.

### Credit and license

Highland communities of the Andes describe this as ancestral knowledge, handed down and kept alive in their own places.

**Quispillaccta** and the **Asociación Bartolomé Aripaylla**, the association of the Machaca sisters, Magdalena, Marcela and Lidia, brought the practice back in their community. Their *crianza del agua* is their own living practice, and is not part of what this entry teaches.

**desco**'s Programa Regional Sur published the build manual, written by Yordy Santa Cruz Cárdenas, Pablo Ordóñez Sánchez, Urbano Jacobo Huamaní and Fernando Camiloaga Jiménez. **PACC Perú** and the authors of its brief, Ronal Cervantes Zavala and Victor Bustinza Urviola, measured what 146 qochas cost and stored.

The desco manual is free to download. It carries no license statement, so UnaMente links to it and does not copy it.
