### The story

Above Huamantanga, people have been steering water into the mountain for a very long time. Researchers who studied the system date it to about 1 400 years ago, before the Inca. Forest Trends' summary of the study calls it one of the last such systems still intact.

The names say what it does. *Mamanteo* comes from the Spanish for breastfeeding: the mountain is fed in the rains so that it can feed the springs later. *Amunas*, as Forest Trends explains the Quechua word, means "to retain."

Not all of it has survived. The researchers counted up to 30 old infiltration canals above Huamantanga, of which 11 were still in use; the rest were no longer in use. There were 14 working ponds, which a regional case report says people call *las lagunas de los abuelos*, "the grandparents' lagoons," and traces of up to 30 more. One canal was restored in 2013, with the community's labor and support from outside organizations.

Huamantanga's own district counts it differently. In a 2012 report, the district's mayor at the time wrote of "the 30 canals and 23 lagoons or infiltration ponds that we now have," and called recovering this knowledge "a challenge for us": "esta tecnología de recarga hídrica, la que heredamos de nuestros antepasados," this technology for recharging water that we inherited from our ancestors.

### The role it plays

In Huamantanga the rains fall from about December to April, according to the researchers; *comuneros* interviewed in 2012 said the rainy season has grown shorter, to about two months now. From June, they said, the water held in the mountain, coming out at the springs, is their only source. The *mamanteo* moves water from the wet season into the dry one: canals carry part of the rain from the ravines onto stony slopes, where it soaks into fractured rock and travels slowly underground to springs lower down.

A team of researchers working with the community measured this. They released a dye into a canal and watched for it at the springs below. On average it took 45 days to come out, and anywhere from two weeks to eight months. From those results, and an assumption about how much of the water comes back, they estimated that the system could raise the local stream's dry-season flow by anywhere from 3% to 554%. That range is very wide, and it rests on that assumption.

The same team estimated what similar systems might do if they were used across the mountains that supply Lima: hold back about 99 million cubic meters of water a year and raise dry-season flows by about 7.5% on average. Those are model estimates for a large area, not something measured at Huamantanga, and the researchers say more study is needed before anyone relies on them.

### How it works, and how the community keeps it alive

**It is kept by communal work.** The researchers describe a set schedule for irrigation. The district's 2012 report says each of the community's two neighborhoods, Anduy and Shihual, has its own water committee, which looks after the water from its source through the canals for the *mamanteo*. *Comuneros* asked for that report said the work is done in the rainy season, as each committee's assembly assigns it. Once, all the *comuneros* went out together in work parties; now two or three go out to each canal. They clear the channel and set small checks of stone or sod across it so that the water spills out over the slope, which they call *extender el agua*, spreading the water. A regional case report by CONDESAN, a research organization, describes communal work on the canals during the rains at least twice a week, with about six *comuneros* sent up each time to "feed" the mountain, taking turns from each section of the community's register, and penalties for those who do not take their turn. The canals are high above the village; the report notes that older *comuneros* can no longer make the climb.

**The knowledge is passed by word of mouth,** from older to younger *comuneros*, often from father to son, according to CONDESAN.

**Ritual was part of it.** The researchers note cultural and religious practices around the upkeep of the system. CONDESAN's report says the ceremonies that once went with the work, as part of a reverence for water, have faded, as has an older custom of going up together to clean the canals before the rains. The 2012 report, in the part drawn from its interviews, describes it: each neighborhood went up before the rains with its music, a *quena* flute and a drum, and when the cleaning was done the women prepared food, spread out on cloths, and people drank and danced. The water festival, with bands and flowered hats, is now mostly the cleaning itself, and the custom, the report says, is being lost.

**The community helped measure it.** The researchers write that the system "was mapped, reconstructed, and monitored together with comuneros," that 117 community members took part in group discussions, interviews and surveys, and they thank residents for their "consent to our work."

### What to understand, and what not to assume

**This is living cultural property, not a blueprint.** The *mamanteo* belongs to the community that keeps it working. This page describes it; it does not teach it, and nothing here is meant to be followed elsewhere.

**It depends on the place.** It works because of Huamantanga's own slopes, rock and springs, and because there is water in the ravines to divert. The researchers say the ground still needs more study. Whether such conditions are common elsewhere is unclear.

**It is under strain.** CONDESAN's report describes canals in poor condition, some abandoned, and upkeep that has become irregular. It names the causes: weaker communal organization, young people leaving for Lima, reliance on dams, knowledge not being passed on, and competition for water between the community's two sections. Silt is filling the ponds. Overgrazing and changing rains add to it. In the 2012 interviews, people said the old lagoons, the grandparents' lagoons with a standing stone, a *huanca*, at their center, had not been cleaned since the 1940s, and that they no longer know how they were used. The report's author describes such stones as sacred to the people of old Huamantanga; those asked in 2012 thought they showed how high the water stood. That is a different picture from the researchers' 14 working ponds; we give both.

**Read the big numbers carefully.** The 45 days is measured. The 3% to 554% and the 99 million cubic meters are estimates from a model, and the researchers present them as possibilities.

### Custodians, and how this entry was made

This knowledge is held by the community of Huamantanga. The nearest thing to its own voice that we have found is a preliminary 2012 report published by Huamantanga's district municipality and the NGO Alternativa, from a project carried out with the Anduy neighborhood's water committee, which includes interviews with local authorities and *comuneros*. We found no custodian-led organization with a public contact page; if you know of one, please tell us.

This entry is written from what outsiders have published: the 2019 study by Boris F. Ochoa-Tocachi and colleagues, with researchers from the Regional Initiative for Hydrological Monitoring of Andean Ecosystems (iMHEA), Imperial College London and other universities, done with the community's participation; Forest Trends' summary of that study; and a case report by Katya Pérez, Junior Gil, Oscar Angulo, Juan Diego Bardales and Javier Antiporta of CONDESAN. The five authors of that report are also among the authors of the study, so these are not two independent accounts. The 2012 report, *El sistema de infiltración hídrica para el mamanteo de Huamantanga*, written by Jorge Avila Cedrón with contributions from Alex Bonilla, presented by Alcides Espinoza, then the district's mayor, was also used. The case report and the 2012 report were read in Castellano; the quotations are translated by us. It is not an endorsement of UnaMente by the community or anyone else.
