### The story

*Fanya juu* means "throw it upwards" in Kiswahili. That is the whole method in two words. A farmer digs a ditch across the slope, along the contour, and throws the soil uphill to make a bank. Grass is planted on the bank to hold it. Rain that runs down the field stops at the bank, and the soil it carries settles behind it. Year after year the strip above each bank fills and flattens, until the slope has become a set of terraces.

Fanya juu terracing began around 1937 in Machakos District, in eastern Kenya, according to the WOCAT record, and came into prominence in the 1950s. FAO's guide says the terraces were first developed in the 1950s. They spread fast in the 1970s and 1980s with Kenya's national soil and water conservation program. The digging was often done by farmers' own self-help groups. By the mid-1990s, when the record's field data were gathered, almost all cropland in Machakos was terraced, according to its compiler. Fanya juu is also used in Ethiopia, Tanzania and Uganda.

### The problem it solves

On a sloping field, rain runs downhill and takes the topsoil with it. The field loses its best soil, the water is gone before the crop can use it, and gullies start. Fanya juu breaks the slope into short sections, so the water slows down, more of it soaks in, and the soil stays in the field.

**Two ways to set them, depending on the rain.** In dry areas the ditches run exactly along the contour, to hold the rain where it falls. In wetter areas they are set on a slight slope, so extra water drains away safely instead of overflowing the bank.

### How it works

Three free guides give the method: the WOCAT record for Kenya, FAO's guide to land management in practice, and a Kiswahili poster from the organization Justdiggit with six steps. In outline:

- **Watch where the water runs** on your field (the poster's first step, *angalia*).
- **Mark the contour lines** with a measuring tool before you dig (*pima*).
- **Space them by the slope.** FAO gives 9 to 20 m between ditches and the Kiswahili poster 10 to 20 m, depending on the slope. FAO's example: on a 15% slope with moderately deep soil, about 12 m apart.
- **Dig the ditch and throw the soil uphill** to form the bank (*chimba*). The WOCAT record and the Kiswahili poster both give a ditch 60 cm deep and 60 cm wide. The record gives a bank 40 cm high and 50 cm to 1 m wide at the base, leveled and packed firm. The poster adds a block 1 m long left undug every 10 m.
- **Leave a small ledge** of 15 to 30 cm between the ditch and the bank, so the soil does not slide back in (FAO).
- **Plant grass on the bank** to hold it (*imarisha*). The WOCAT record names Napier grass, or makarikari grass in drier zones, so the bank also feeds animals. Fruit or other useful trees can go just above the bank, or bananas and pawpaws in the ditch in drier areas, where the runoff collects.
- **Lay out in the dry season, plant the grass when the rains begin** (WOCAT).

Build it from the guides, not from this summary. They are linked below.

### What you need

- A hoe and a shovel, and a measuring tool to find the contour.
- Grass cuttings or other plants with strong roots for the banks.
- **People.** The poster's third step is "find people to help you dig" (*tafuta nguvu kazi*). The poster suggests a group that digs each member's field in turn. In Kenya the terraces were often built by self-help groups (FAO).

**The work.** This is heavy hand digging, done in the dry season. In the Kenya records (WOCAT and FAO), building the terraces took about **ninety (90) person-days per hectare** on a typical 15% slope, and more on steeper hillsides, where the ditches are closer together. Keeping them up took about **ten (10) person-days per hectare a year**. The farmers bore the whole cost. The guides suggest spreading the building over several years and working in groups.

### What to expect, and what not to expect

**Higher yields, in the studies FAO gathered.** For maize in Kenya, FAO's guide gives yields of 2.1 to 3.4 metric tons per hectare without terraces and 3.1 to 4.5 with fanya juu. For beans in Tanzania, 1.5 to 1.8 without and 2.8 with. In its Kenya case, yields rose by about 25%. These are figures compiled by FAO from other studies; we have not read the studies themselves.

**Not straight away.** From the farmers' point of view, FAO's Kenya case rates the return on building the terraces as slightly negative in the short term and positive in the long term. The bank takes some land out of crops, and the payback comes over years.

**Less soil lost, more water held.** The Kenya records list less soil loss and runoff, more soil moisture in dry areas and better drainage in wetter ones. Over the years the strips level out: in the record's example, a 15% slope became about 3%. The records give no measured soil loss figure we could use.

### When it does not work

**Where something simpler would do.** The WOCAT record says to build fanya juu only where it is really needed, where mulching, contour plowing or grass strips are not enough to hold the runoff. The bank takes land out of crops, and it makes walking and carrying loads across the field more awkward.

**On stony soils,** where the ditch cannot be dug (FAO).

**In very dry areas,** unless the terraces are built to harvest water. FAO says fanya juu is not suited to dry areas otherwise.

**If the layout or the bank is poor.** Badly laid out or loosely packed banks break in heavy rain and can cause more erosion. Accurate contour marking and well packed banks are the fix.

**If the bank is left bare.** Unplanted banks wash away.

**Where water pools.** Water can collect above the bank and waterlog the crop (FAO).

**Where the grass competes with the crop.** Grass on the bank can shade or crowd the crop beside it unless it is cut back regularly.

### What keeps it running

The upkeep is every year: build the bank up from below, dig out the soil that washed into the ditch and throw it uphill (the Kiswahili poster says *kila mwaka*, each year; the WOCAT record says after heavy rain), repair breaks, and trim the grass to keep it dense. In the Kenya records that is about ten person-days per hectare a year, by the farmer, with hand tools; no outside skill or parts are needed. The WOCAT record's compiler estimates that about 150 000 households across 3 000 km² use the terraces, that about 70% took them up with no outside material support, and that they are spreading on their own, especially through women's groups. Those are estimates of spread from 1995 field work, not a count of terraces still kept up. Nobody we read has measured what share of terraces built under the national program are still maintained today. Still in use, years later: not measured.

### Where it has been used

Kenya, where it began, in Machakos, and spread first; also Ethiopia, Tanzania and Uganda, according to FAO.
