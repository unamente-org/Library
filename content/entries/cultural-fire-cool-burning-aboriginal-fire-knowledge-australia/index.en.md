### The story

In Cape York, Kuku Thaypan Elders Dr. Musgrave and Dr. George led the revival of their fire practices and passed their knowledge to Victor Steffensen. He has spent decades encouraging the revival of cultural burning and has run workshops all around the country. The registrar's profile quotes the way it spreads: "community mentoring community."

The communities drawn in by those workshops came together as Firesticks, registered in 2018. Its work has been in overdrive, as the registrar of Indigenous corporations put it, since the fires of the 2019 to 2020 summer. Firesticks wrote at the end of 2020 that at times the call for help had been overwhelming.

On 24 June 2026, Firesticks became Living Knowledge Network. The organization says Cultural Fire was always one part of a wider system of Traditional Knowledge, and that its work now reaches beyond fire, into food, plants, health, land and schools. In Victor Steffensen's words: "Cultural Fire was the starting point, but the vision was always bigger."

### The role it plays

In its own submission to the 2020 New South Wales bushfire inquiry, Firesticks described its work as managing Country by burning, and revitalizing Country and knowledge by building community networks and recording cultural knowledge. It described itself as an Indigenous-led network of cultural fire knowledge holders and practitioners who, together with Indigenous communities, are custodians of traditional knowledge of Country and of the use of fire to keep landscapes healthy. It said it values and respects Country, local knowledge, and the protocols of Elders and ancestors.

It is not presented as a fix brought in from outside. Oliver Costello, a Bundjalung man who was then Firesticks' chief executive, put it this way: people ask how to burn, but cultural fire is "a cultural framework for living."

### How it works, and how the communities keep it alive

**Reading Country comes first.** The 2020 submission names it: learning by observation, mentoring and sharing of knowledge on Country. Peter Townsend, a Wiradjuri, Wayilwan and Gomeroi lead fire practitioner, says reading Country is literal: "we come out and read it and look at it."

**How a cool burn looks,** as the registrar of Indigenous corporations described it in 2022: the flames never reach the treetops, what is left is charcoal rather than pale gray ash, the smoke is pale and clears quickly, and new shoots appear within a couple of weeks.

**The knowledge passes from person to person, on Country.** Living Knowledge Network reports that since 2020 it has mentored more than 750 Indigenous community members in traditional land management and delivered more than 300 Indigenous land management workshops across the country. These are its own figures. As Victor Steffensen says: "The knowledge is in the landscape."

### What to understand, and what not to assume

**This is living cultural property, not a blueprint.** The knowledge of when and how to burn a place belongs to the Traditional Owners of that Country, and is learned on that Country with them. Living Knowledge Network is a network of many peoples and Countries, each with its own fire knowledge. It is not one central authority, and this entry does not speak for any of them.

**This page describes; it does not teach.** Nothing here is meant to be followed. Burning land without the cultural connection, the reading of Country and the mentoring the practice requires is not Cultural Fire, and it is not what this entry offers.

**It is a revival under way.** The network describes its work as revitalizing Cultural Fire practice, and the registrar described it as re-establishing cultural burning around the country. It is being rebuilt community by community, not restored everywhere at once.

### Custodians, and how this entry was made

This knowledge is held by Aboriginal Traditional Owner communities. The custodian-led organization to turn to is **Living Knowledge Network**, through the [contact page on its own site](https://livingknowledge.network/connect/).
