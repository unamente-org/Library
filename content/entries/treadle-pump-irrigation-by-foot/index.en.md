*Groundwater sat just metres below the fields — but without power or fuel, farmers couldn't reach it. The answer was a pump you drive with your own legs.*

**At a glance**

•      Origin: northwest Bangladesh, late 1970s — engineer Gunnar Barnes with the Rangpur Dinajpur Rural Service (RDRS); scaled by Paul Polak's iDE.

•      Maturity: Scaled — over 1.5 million sold in Bangladesh alone; replicated across Asia and Africa.

•      Energy: none but human. Powered like a StairMaster — the rhythm of your legs drives the pistons.

•      Licence: Open — the core design is not patented.

**The story**

In northwest Bangladesh in the late 1970s, small farmers faced a cruel paradox: groundwater lay a few metres beneath their feet, but without electricity or fuel they couldn't reach it. In the dry season, crops failed — and landless farmers rented water from big landholders at prices that kept them poor.

A Norwegian engineer, Gunnar Barnes, working with the RDRS, developed the model RDRS introduced in December 1980 — a foot-powered device that used the weight and rhythm of a person's legs to drive pistons and pull water from shallow aquifers. Then, in the early 1980s, Paul Polak of iDE saw it and recognised the real gap: the technology existed, but the market didn't. iDE built the network of local manufacturers, dealers, and installers that could put the pump in farmers' hands at a price they could actually afford.

Over 12 years, 1.5 million pumps were sold in Bangladesh. The programme cost \$12 million (12 000 000) — where conventional dams and canals to irrigate the same land would have cost around \$1.5 billion (1 500 000 000). During the programme, farmers earned an estimated \$150 million (150 000 000) a year in additional net income, according to a 2012 account first published by National Geographic and republished by the Post Carbon Institute.

*It changed more than economics. Where large landholders had monopolised water and charged high fees, the treadle pump gave small farmers their own source — quietly restructuring who held power in the village.*

**The problem it solves**

Smallholder farmers with shallow groundwater but no electricity or fuel can't irrigate in the dry season — so crops fail, or they pay exploitative rates to rent water from those who can pump it.

The treadle pump turns a person's own effort into reliable irrigation, at a price a small farmer can own outright.

**How it works**

The user stands on two treadles and steps as if climbing stairs. That motion drives two pistons in cylinders; the pistons create suction that lifts water up an intake pipe, and pushes it out through a delivery pipe to the field or channel.

It's a suction pump, so it works best where the water table is within about 7 metres of the surface. The original could be built largely from bamboo and local materials; sturdier manufactured metal versions last longer.

**What to expect — and what not to**

A capable pump for shallow-water smallholdings — a person can irrigate roughly half an acre to an acre depending on conditions.

•      Needs groundwater within reach of suction (about 7m) — deeper water needs a different tool.

•      It is human-powered, so it takes real physical effort; output depends on how long someone can treadle.

•      Not a substitute for large-scale irrigation, and not suited to deep water tables.

**When it doesn't work — the honest part**

The honest limit is bodily: this runs on human energy, and there's only so much treadling a person can do in a day, especially in heat. It suits small plots, not large farms.

And its history carries the same lesson UnaMente keeps finding: the pump succeeded not because the device was clever but because iDE built the market and supply chain around it. A good tool with no way to buy, repair, or replace it doesn't spread. Plan for the supply chain, not just the pump.

**Credit & licence**

Original design by Gunnar Barnes with the Rangpur Dinajpur Rural Service (RDRS), Bangladesh; scaled through the market-building work of Paul Polak and International Development Enterprises (iDE). The core design is unpatented and open.

For the commercial-scale version, KickStart International makes the well-known MoneyMaker pump; the open DIY design is documented on Akvopedia.
