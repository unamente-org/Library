**Category:** Soil and food · **Origin:** Maradi and Tillabery regions, Niger — women farmers organised through FUMA Gaskiya · **Maturity:** Proven, in its own setting · **Trust tier:** Moderate · **Licence:** Owned by no one · **Entry date:** 12 November 2026 · **Documented by:** UnaMente

### The story

In villages around Maradi, in southern Niger, women farmers worked out something their soil badly needed and nobody was selling them. Human urine, collected and left to stand in a sealed container for a couple of months, is a nitrogen fertilizer.

They gave it a name borrowed from elsewhere. *Oga* means "the boss" in Igbo, a language of south-east Nigeria — about a thousand kilometres from Maradi — where it is everyday office usage for someone in authority. Applying urine is taboo among some groups in the region, and the published account says the name was proposed by a farmer as a replacement. It is not an abbreviation and it stands for nothing: it is simply a word that let people talk about the thing. That is not decoration. A practice you cannot say out loud is a practice you cannot teach your neighbour.

The sequence matters, because it is the opposite of the usual one. The farmers were already doing this. Researchers did not arrive with the idea; they arrived and documented one that was working, which is why the paper that came out of it calls Oga an "auto-innovation" — an innovation from the farmers themselves.

Between 2014 and 2016, women across two regions ran 681 trials on their own fields, and the results were measured alongside them: 159 trials in the first year, 288 in the second, 234 in the third. Participation grew from roughly 160 women to more than 230. The work was organised through FUMA Gaskiya — the Fédération des Unions de producteurs du Maradi — a farmers' federation of more than 12 000 members, more than half of them women. Ali M. Aminou of the federation is a co-author of the published study, alongside lead author Hannatou O. Moussa of INRAN, Niger's own national agricultural research institute, in Maradi, with Nwankwo, Stern, Haussmann and Herrmann. The credit belongs to the federation and its women members first, and to the research team for doing the less glamorous work of measuring carefully what those women already knew.

### The problem it solves

Sahelian soils are short of nitrogen, and pearl millet — the staple the region depends on — grows poorly without it. Mineral fertilizer is the obvious answer and often not a reachable one. Oga addresses exactly that gap, using something every household already produces and otherwise discards.

### What you need

A container that seals properly, somewhere out of the way to leave it standing for two to three months, something to measure and pour with, and water if the soil is dry when you apply it. No specialist skill, no equipment to buy beyond containers.

There is no monetary cost for the fertilizer itself, and that is genuinely the point of it — but the practice is not free. It asks for containers, storage space in a household that may not have spare space, the carrying of urine and water out to the field, and the labour of two applications in a season on top of everything else a farming day already contains. The published study did no financial evaluation at all, so nobody has yet put a figure on that effort. Treat the cost as real work rather than as nothing.

### How to do it

This is the protocol as the published study gives it. Where a summary brief of the same work differs, the study is the primary source and this follows the study.

1.  **Collect urine, and keep faeces out entirely.** This is the one line that is not negotiable. Urine is the comparatively low-risk fraction; contamination with faeces changes the safety picture completely and demands far more treatment.

2.  **Store it undiluted in a sealed container for at least two to three months.** The Niger trials stored it at ambient temperatures of 22–24 °C, following published sanitisation guidance on storage. Sealing matters twice over: it limits pathogens and it keeps the nitrogen from escaping.

3.  **Apply twice in the season** — fourteen (14) days after sowing, and again thirty-five (35) days after sowing.

4.  **If the soil is moist, apply undiluted:** two hundred millilitres (0.2 litres) into each planting pocket.

5.  **If the soil is dry, dilute one part urine to one part water, and apply more:** four hundred millilitres (0.4 litres) per planting pocket.

Two things worth knowing if you are working outside the Niger setting. Guidance from Schönning and Stenström, published through the Stockholm Environment Institute's EcoSanRes programme in both English and French, gives storage as one to six months at 4–20 °C depending on the crop, and adds a rule the Niger protocol did not need to state: a single household using its own urine on its own food can skip the storage period, provided the last application is at least one month before harvest — the risk between family members is already higher than anything the crop adds. For crops eaten raw where the edible part grows above the ground, the same guidance says to work the urine into the soil rather than leaving it on the surface. The Rich Earth Institute's farmer guide, written for conditions in the United States, gives sealed storage at 20 °C or above for one to six months, and says plainly that this is not a method any US federal agency has formally recognised. Those three storage figures do not agree with each other. Warmer storage sanitises faster; the honest summary is a range, not a number, and the one-month pre-harvest rule is the most useful single safeguard for anyone applying this to food crops.

If your rules are not Niger's, check them. In the United States, for instance, the position is genuinely unsettled: urine is neither explicitly permitted nor prohibited under the national organic programme, individual state certifiers decide for themselves, and whether produce-safety rules restrict its use on crops eaten raw is ambiguous.

### What to expect — and what not to expect

**When it works well.** Across the three seasons of trials, pearl millet **panicle** yield rose by about 30% — roughly 200 to 300 kilograms more per hectare. Year by year, the gain was 254 kg per hectare in 2014, 193 in 2015, and 288 in 2016; the study reports that 2016 figure in one place as 288 kg per hectare and elsewhere as a range of 170 to 313, so both belong here. Relative gains ran from 26% to 38% at Serkin Haussa and around 40% at Safo.

The single most useful finding is where those gains landed: **they were largest on the fields that produced least without it.** This practice helps most exactly where the soil is worst, which is the opposite of how most agricultural inputs behave.

**One word to be careful about, because every retelling drops it.** What was measured is panicle yield, not grain. The authors say panicle yield correlates closely with grain yield, and that is a reasonable thing to say — but grain in the sack was never weighed in these trials. Anyone reading "30% more millet" should know that is an inference from the panicles, not a measurement of the harvest.

**What it supplies.** Two applications are estimated to deliver roughly 10 kilograms of nitrogen, 0.8 kilograms of phosphorus and 4 kilograms of potassium per hectare — estimated, because the urine itself was not analysed in these trials. Rich Earth's guide gives typical urine nitrogen content as about 0.57%. The shape of that is what matters: this is overwhelmingly a nitrogen source.

**Conditions it needs.** Rainfed pearl millet in Sahelian conditions is what has actually been tested. Storage time has to be allowed for. The soil's moisture at the time of application decides the dose. And the practice has to be socially acceptable where it is used — in the Niger setting the researchers consulted religious leaders and medical experts and found no religious, cultural, social or sanitary objection, but that is a finding about that setting, not a general one.

**Where it does not work.** It is nitrogen, not a complete fertilizer. On ground that is short of phosphorus or potassium, Oga alone will disappoint, and no amount of it will fix a phosphorus problem. Urine is also salty, so repeated heavy application to the same bed builds up salt over time.

**Not suitable for.** Any use where faeces have entered the container. And for anything eaten raw, treat the one-month-before-harvest rule as the floor and work the urine into the soil rather than applying it to the surface — the American guide gives no pre-harvest interval at all, which is a real gap in it.

**Common failures, and how to avoid them.** Cutting the storage time short is the first one: the months of standing are the treatment, not a delay before it. Mixing in faeces is the second, and it is not recoverable by storing longer. The third is a dosing error that is easy to make from a summary rather than the protocol: a widely circulated brief of this same work gives the diluted mixture at the undiluted dose, which delivers about half the nitrogen the study's dry-soil protocol intends. Dilute one to one and apply the larger dose, or apply undiluted at the smaller one. The fourth is an unsealed container, which loses nitrogen to the air and wastes the point of storing it.

**How certain this is, honestly.** One study, one crop, one country, three seasons. The authors are candid about the mess inside their own data: whole villages are sometimes missing from a season, some farmers in the first year departed from the protocol and that year's results varied widely, an interaction between treatment and rainfall is unexplained, and — the caveat that deserves most weight — treatment type was not a significant factor in their multivariate analysis, where season, village and soil type dominated. That does not void the result. It does mean "30% from Oga" is a headline drawn from a messier picture than the headline sounds, and anyone depending on this should know that.

### Where it has been replicated

No outcome data from outside Niger has been published that we could find. The practice has been actively shared beyond the Sahel — the research programme behind the Niger work has published video material in Luo and Swahili showing FUMA Gaskiya's method, aimed at East African farmers — but sharing a method is not the same as measuring what it did somewhere else, and we are not going to present the one as the other.

### An open question we would like answered

The Niger protocol is tuned to pearl millet in Sahelian conditions. As far as we can find, nobody has published whether the same timing and doses hold for maize, for vegetables, or for Andean or South Asian crops. Rich Earth lists crops that performed comparably to synthetic fertilizer in various studies — amaranth, cabbage, corn, cucumber, barley, hay, okra, rice, tomato — which suggests the useful range is wide, but those were separate trials with their own protocols, not this one travelling. The narrow range of the evidence looks like an accident of where the research happened rather than a real limit of the technique. If you have tried this on another crop and measured what happened, we would like to hear from you.
