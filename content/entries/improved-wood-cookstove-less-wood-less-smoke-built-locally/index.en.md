Around three billion people still cook over an open fire, breathing the smoke and spending hours gathering the wood. An improved wood stove built on “rocket” principles makes a small, fiercely hot, well-insulated fire and squeezes the heat into the pot — so it burns less wood, and, with a chimney, carries smoke out of the kitchen.

**At a glance** Origin: rocket-combustion principles from the Aprovecho Research Center (USA); regional designs such as Mexico’s Patsari and Central America’s Justa · Maturity: Scaled · Effort: local materials and skilled building; daily fuel preparation is real labor · Licence: open.

**How it works.** The core ideas (set out by Larry Winiarski at Aprovecho): insulate tightly around the fire with lightweight material; put a short internal chimney right above the flames so the gases burn more completely; feed the sticks in a few at a time; keep a strong draft; and size the gap around the pot so the hot gases scrub heat into it. Build it to a full illustrated guide, not to this summary.

**What to expect — and what it does NOT do.** Built and used well, these stoves cut fuelwood use substantially — Mexico’s Patsari is reported to roughly halve it — and a good chimney model cuts indoor smoke markedly (Patsari, about two-thirds less fine particles and carbon monoxide). But the honest catch is the gap between the lab and the real kitchen: in field studies, stoves emit far more than in controlled tests — in one study in Malawi, close to ten times more — because households burn big logs instead of chopped sticks, operate the stove differently, and above all keep the old open fire going alongside the new one (“stove stacking”). An improved wood stove is also not a clean stove — it reduces smoke, it does not remove it — and health benefits in practice have been mixed. It fails when built without insulation, when the chimney isn’t cleaned, when the fuel isn’t prepared, or when it sits beside a still-used open fire.

**Who made it.** Larry Winiarski and the Aprovecho Research Center for the rocket-combustion principles; GIRA in Mexico — Omar Masera, Víctor Berrueta and Rufus Edwards — for the Patsari (whose name means roughly “the one who keeps and cares for” in the Purépecha language); and the many regional builders of the Justa and other adaptations.
